What this settles

The PuzzleBoard decoder refuses to decode a fragment narrower than min_window corners, because below some size a fragment does not identify its own position on the 501×501 master pattern uniquely. That constant was set from a spot check on seven planted origins. This survey computes the answer exactly, over every one of the 251 001 positions, for the shipped board and for half a million alternatives drawn uniformly from the same construction.

Three results matter to the library. The shipped min_window = 7 is exactly the floor — not a conservative choice with slack, and not lowerable. The switch to rotations-only decoding is what made it sound: while the decoder searched all eight dihedral relabellings, the shipped constant sat two rings below the provable floor. And a better board is not reachable by sampling, though the space is far too large for that to settle whether one exists.

The reduction that makes this cheap

A board is two cyclic sequences superposed — map A drives the vertical edge dots, map B the horizontal ones. Because their periods are coprime with the map height, the code table factorises: what a fragment at master position (i, j) sees splits into a part that depends only on an index u into map A and a part that depends only on an index v into map B.

The consequence is that the number of hypotheses a fragment admits is a sum of rank-one outer products, one per rotation:

hypotheses(u, v) = Σg rowg[u] · colg[v]

The identity contributes exactly 1 everywhere — every fragment matches itself. So a position is ambiguous exactly when some non-identity rotation has both factors non-zero there, the ambiguous set is a union of combinatorial rectangles, and a board is perfect if and only if every non-identity rotation has one factor identically zero. Verified against an independent per-position hypothesis count.

Why this collapses the search

The half turn is the only rotation that does not exchange the two code maps, so its two factors are functions of one ring each. A ring whose factor vanishes kills the half-turn term of any board it joins — whatever it is paired with. That turns one axis of a product search over ~10193 pairs into a linear scan over rings. The quarter turns do mix the maps and stay irreducibly pairwise.

Read the last two columns against the shipped min_window = 7. Under C4 it is the smallest span that is perfect — exactly the floor. Under D4 that same span leaves 504 positions ambiguous, and the D4 floor is 9. So for as long as the decoder searched all eight dihedral relabellings, the shipped constant admitted fragments that were not provably unique, and the runtime uniqueness gate had to decline them — paying in recall for hypotheses a camera can never observe. Restricting the search to the four rotations did not merely halve the work; it moved the constant from two rings below the floor to sitting on it.

How the shipped board compares to 500 000 alternatives

Every valid board is a pair of closed trails in a 24-vertex, 168-edge quotient graph; there are about 1096 such rings and 10193 pairs. Rings can be drawn uniformly from that space, so "is the shipped board special?" is directly measurable.

The per-ring statistic is the half-turn support: how many master offsets of a ring collide with their own half turn. Zero is the good case.

Two independent surveys agreeing

The ring survey says 1.43 % of rings are half-turn-clean at span 7, which predicts 1 − (1 − 0.0143)² = 2.85 % of pairs; the pair survey measured 2.91 % directly. Multiplying the two independent pair conditions predicts 0.51 % perfect; 0.48 % was measured. The reduction and the direct tally were computed by different code paths.

Could a board reach six corners?

This is the question with something at stake: min_window = 6 would accept materially smaller fragments, which on partially occluded views is recall. Nothing in 40 000 pairs came close, and the binding constraint is the per-ring one — a span-6 board needs a span-6 half-turn-clean ring, and none of 500 000 rings was clean.

The left tail of that distribution says how far away it is. Support values are always multiples of three, an artefact of the row-rotation orbit structure:

The tail thins by roughly 2.5× per step of three. Extrapolating the three steps from 9 down to 0 puts P(a random ring is span-6-clean) near 10−7. That is an extrapolation across three unobserved bins, not a measurement — but it points somewhere useful: 10−7 is out of reach for uniform sampling and well within reach of a directed search. Against a space of ~1096 rings, it also implies such rings exist in enormous numbers.

What is not yet known

A span-6-clean ring is necessary, not sufficient — the quarter-turn condition must hold at span 6 as well, and it was never met in 40 000 pairs either. Whether the two can be satisfied together is open, and is the next thing to run: hill-climbing on the per-ring support, then the quarter-turn test on the survivors. Until that runs, the honest statement is that no better board is known, not that none exists.

Beyond uniqueness

Uniqueness is binary and worst-case. Three other properties turned out to matter, and they do not rank boards the same way.

Error margin — the constraint nobody set

The minimum Hamming distance between the codes of two master positions is what decides how many misread dots a decode survives. At the shipped operating point it is 2: enough to notice one bad dot, never enough to correct it.

The span-7 value is structural, not luck. At that span every informative code row is read exactly twice, so flipping any single code bit changes exactly two observed dots — which forces dmin ≥ 2, and the bound is attained. At spans 5 and 6 some row is read only once, which is why a single misread dot can move you to a different position there.

Note the last row: at span 9 the shipped board has the lowest minimum distance of the seven boards compared. Uniqueness and error margin are genuinely different axes, and the shipped board is optimised for the first. A search that ranks only on uniqueness would inherit the same blind spot.

Dot balance — fixed by the construction

Across all 500 000 rings the number of set dots was 250 or 251 out of 501, never anything else. The shipped rings are 251 and 250. Balance is forced by the construction, so it is not a design variable and not worth ranking on.

Longest dot run — a free choice nobody has made

The longest run of identical dots along a map row ranged from 5 to 24 over the sample, median 9. Both shipped rings are 8 — slightly better than typical, well short of the best seen. Long runs are locally featureless under the sampler, so this is a real quality axis that is currently unoptimised and costs nothing to include in a search objective.

What follows for the library

  • Keep min_window = 7. It is the exact C4 floor for the shipped board, now verified over all 251 001 positions rather than seven planted origins. Lowering it to 6 would admit 3 330 ambiguous positions.
  • The rotations-only default is load-bearing. Under D4 the same constant is two rings below the floor. If reflection search is ever re-enabled by default, the constant must go to 9 with it.
  • There is no error-correction margin at the minimum window — dmin is 2 there, by construction. Whatever robustness the decoder shows in practice comes from fragments larger than the minimum, which is worth stating where max_bit_error_rate is documented.
  • A better board is a research result, not a config change. Boards printed against a different code do not decode against the reference implementation, so any replacement is opt-in and additive at best.

Method. Rings drawn uniformly by Wilson's algorithm → BEST → Hierholzer over the σ-quotient of the letter de Bruijn graph. Every uniqueness figure is exact over all 251 001 master positions — no sampling of positions anywhere — via the rank-one factorisation, which is checked elementwise against a brute-force evaluator that materialises the master cell by cell and shares none of its assumptions. Counts are stored as integers with their denominator; 99.9996 % rounds to 100 % and would answer the central question wrong.
